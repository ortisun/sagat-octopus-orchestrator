# Research & Decisions: Triggers, webhooks, messaging and correlation

## Main decision

Event Gateway with Inbox/Outbox in Postgres; subscription matcher partitioned by tenant+type+key; external Kafka/SQS adapters with idempotent checkpoints.

## Rationale

Receive and send events with dedupe, persistence and delivery controls, waking durable workflows correctly. The decision respects the recovery, auditability, security and versioning properties defined in the Constitution.

## Alternatives considered

1. **Generic ad hoc queue-based solution:** rejected as the sole durability mechanism, since message delivery is not equivalent to a transaction over workflow state.
2. **Independent services per feature from day 1:** postponed to avoid operational and consistency complexity without a benchmark.
3. **SDK-specific logic:** rejected for core invariants, to avoid behavior differences between languages.

## Risks and mitigation

- Complexity/scope risk: deliver P1 user stories separately, running contract tests.
- Semantic ambiguity risk: encode reducers and golden fixtures, do not decide via UI alone.
- Crash/redelivery risk: test harness with real failures at checkpoints.
- Security risk: specific pentest and tenant isolation from the first milestone.

## Questions for spike validation

- Latency and overhead benchmarks on the critical path defined for this feature.
- Detail compliance and retention requirements according to the deployment environment.

## Decisions taken in this feature

- **D78 — The inbox and the matcher are the platform's own code over its own database; broker clients are an adapter crate.** ADR-0012. The domain holds the envelope, the limits, the verdicts and the schedules; the application the services and the ports; `infra-postgres` the SQL; `infra-connectors` the broker libraries and the guarded sender; `scheduler` the loops. Nothing in the domain or the application names a broker, a socket or `reqwest`. (G73.)
- **D79 — An event is identified by `(tenant, namespace, source, event id)`, and the inbox row is the deduplication.** The same id with the same content (a SHA-256 of type, subject, correlation key and body) is `DUPLICATE` and still `202`; with other content it is `409 EVENT_ID_REUSED` and nothing is written (audited once per endpoint and reason). The window comes from the database's clock; after it the id is a new event (and, D84, starts its runs again); an event that has not been dispatched is not forgotten with its window. A source is the slug of an endpoint or a connector, `api` or `cron`; the last two are reserved, and a slug is unique among the endpoints and connectors of a namespace (one lock decides).
- **D80 — A signed request can be replayed for twice the skew, so an event is remembered for twice the skew and a minute more.** The signature is accepted for `skew` after its timestamp and the timestamp may be `skew` ahead, so a copy can arrive up to `2 × skew` after the first; the margin is for replicas whose clocks are not the database's. `EndpointLimits` refuses a shorter window when the endpoint is saved (default skew 300 s, window 24 h, bounds 1 s to 1 h and 1 minute to 30 days). An event published through the API is not signed and has no skew: its window only has to be inside the bounds. (G93.)
- **D81 — The signature covers everything that gives an event its meaning, and its cost is bounded before anything is computed.** `v1=` and the hex HMAC-SHA256 of `octopus-event-in-v1\n{timestamp}\n{id}\n{type}\n{subject}\n{key}\n` and the body (`…-out-v1` for what the platform sends). The type picks the triggers that fire and the key the waits that wake, so they are signed; the label means a delivery cannot be replayed to an inbound endpoint that shares its secret; no covered field can contain a newline, so the pieces cannot be shifted into one another; a secret under 16 bytes signs and verifies nothing. Before a secret is read or a hash computed, the header is refused if it is over 512 bytes or carries more than four signatures, or the timestamp is outside the window; the body is hashed once per secret, not once per candidate. Verification tries the three newest versions of the secret (rotation), compares in constant time and answers **one** `401` for everything that does not verify (unknown endpoint, bad or stale signature, missing headers, no usable secret). A database error while verifying is a `5xx`, never a verdict on the request. Known vectors are in the unit tests and the runbook. (G75, G92.)
- **D82 — Ingress costs a caller who has proved nothing almost nothing.** A bound on attempts at ten times the endpoint's rate (burst twenty times) is taken before the database is touched beyond the endpoint, the endpoint's own rate after the signature verified, a body over the endpoint's limit is refused before it is verified, and a refusal is audited once per endpoint and reason in a window (a caller can repeat it as often as they like). Unknown endpoints get no bucket. (G78.) Measured: a refusal is cheaper than an admission (`tests/performance/reports/004-events-webhooks-messaging.md`).
- **D83 — Admission is durable before it is acknowledged; matching is asynchronous.** `202` means the inbox row is committed. A backlog of 10 000 undispatched events for a tenant is the limit past which ingress answers `429` with `Retry-After` and the connectors leave messages with their brokers; a duplicate is still answered while the backlog is full.
- **D84 — The dispatcher claims with a lease and records every effect with its delivery in one transaction.** A run started by a trigger is created under the scope `trigger:{id}` and a key derived from the namespace, the source, the event id **and the instant the event was admitted** (an id used again after its window is a new event and must start its run again); a wait is woken in the transaction that writes the delivery `wait:{run}/{node}`; so a dispatcher that dies half way, or two that race, repeat nothing that was recorded. Every consumer of an event is tried even when another fails; the first failure is what the event is retried for, and the retry repeats only what was not recorded. A failure that is only a lack of room (a quota, a store that cannot be reached) does not use up the event's attempts and is retried about every 30 s for a day, then set aside; anything else backs off from a second to ten minutes and is set aside after 12 attempts. A `DEAD` event keeps its payload and its reason (a class, never a detail) for inspection until its window ends.
- **D85 — Triggers belong to the active version of a workflow, with ids that survive republishing.** `event` (type, optional source), `webhook` (an endpoint's slug), `message` (a connector's slug), `cron` and `manual`; registered when a version is published, in the same transaction, and removed when a later version leaves them out. The id is a hash of the workflow and of what the trigger is about (not of its filter), so republishing a version that keeps a trigger does not start a second run for an event already processed. A filter is a side-effect-free expression over `event` and `data`; one that cannot be evaluated on an event does not match it.
- **D86 — A wait and an event meet from both directions, so no order loses one.** A wait records `event_since` (when it began, less its `lookbackMs`) and the dispatcher looks, for every wait it has not looked at, for an event of its type and key received since then and not yet delivered to it; an event looks for the waits it is about. Each search runs in its own transaction and takes only its own row lock; the delivery row makes the second find the first. Waits that began before the migration are given a start and marked searched (G95). The race between an event and the wait's timeout is decided by who commits first (feature 002's lock); the loser is a duplicate and the event is not recorded as delivered to a wait that timed out.
- **D87 — Cron is the dispatcher's, keyed by the tick.** A trigger is due by the database's clock and everything that follows from that (how late it is, the next tick) is worked out from it, not from the replica's. The tick starts its run under `cron-{epoch}` and only then moves `next_fire_at` with a compare-and-set, so two dispatchers that find it due together start one run, and one that dies in between leaves a tick the next finds already started. A tick that cannot start stays the tick that is owed and is tried again about 30 s later under the same key (`retry_at`), so a start that did commit but was not seen to cannot start twice. Missed ticks coalesce (G84). The input of the run is `{event: {source: "cron", type: "cron.fired", …}, data: {scheduledAt, firedAt}}`.
- **D88 — Outgoing deliveries are rows written with the fact they tell, and everything about sending them is in the data.** One row per subscription is inserted in the transaction that commits `run.finished` (ids are UUIDv7, so a listing by id is a listing by age); a subscription that already has 10 000 waiting is switched off instead of given another (audited, G94). A worker claims per subscription, and the subscription's `next_slot_at` moves by `taken / rate` seconds with every claim, so the rate holds across workers and replicas (a claim takes at most a quarter of a second's rate, 25 at most). An attempt is recorded together with the new state of the delivery and the health of the subscription, **fenced by the attempt number**: a worker that was too slow and finds its delivery moved on records nothing. A 2xx is a delivery; 408, 425, 429, 5xx, a timeout, a network failure and a destination the policy refused are retried (5 s, 30 s, 2 min, 10 min, 30 min, 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 24 h, each ±20 %, `Retry-After` honoured up to six hours and never shortening the schedule; 12 attempts, about three days); anything else is `DEAD`. The receiver's answer is never read (only status and headers), stored or logged. Twenty `DEAD` in a row switch a subscription off (counted under a row lock, audited); setting it `ACTIVE` again forgets the failures and puts up to 1 000 dead deliveries back, and only for a subscription the platform switched off (a pause and a resume change nothing that was set aside). Redrive numbers attempts on from where they were. A read-only role sees where a subscription goes but not the path or query of its URL (D93).
- **D89 — Everything the platform sends goes through the egress policy of the namespace, again at every attempt.** The guarded client resolves the name, checks every address, connects to the address it checked, never follows a redirect for a `POST`, and no policy can allow loopback, link-local, private, metadata, multicast or reserved space (feature 009). A refusal is audited without path or query and retried like a network failure, so a policy that is fixed lets the delivery through.
- **D90 — A connector acknowledges only what is in the inbox, and one bad message holds nothing up.** NATS is acknowledged, an SQS message deleted and a Kafka position moved after the event is committed; the position moves in the same transaction, with a compare-and-set that fences a reader that lost its lease; partitions are leased (30 s, renewed between messages as well as between fetches); a message that cannot become an event (no type, not a JSON object, too large, a bad envelope, an id reused for another body) is set aside as a dead letter in the transaction that moves the position, the first thousand per connector being kept (64 KiB each, a message set aside twice once, not counted twice). A full backlog leaves messages with the broker. Credentials are a secret of the namespace and are never sent to a server whose address is not `tls://`; a topic with more than 256 partitions is refused; a NATS reader bounds what it has delivered and not acknowledged by what it can take in half the acknowledgement wait; an SQS reader reads with a limit of its own (16 MiB: ten messages of 256 KiB in XML), holds off longer each time it leaves messages in the queue, and gives up after five failed calls in a row so that the supervisor starts it again with fresh credentials and policy; the supervisor gives a reader 30 s to stop and then abandons it. (G76, G81, G82.)
- **D91 — Three permissions, three quotas.** `event:publish` (Operator, Developer), `event:read` (Viewer, Operator, Developer) and `event:manage` (Developer); `event_endpoints` (100), `webhook_subscriptions` (50) and `event_connectors` (20) per namespace, counted under a lock in the creating transaction like feature 009's. Creating an endpoint, a subscription or a connector needs permission to use the secret it names.
- **D92 — The messages the platform sends and receives only grow within a version.** `events.asyncapi.yaml` (0.2.0) describes the webhooks in both directions, the broker envelope and the event document; `events.baseline.json` holds the shape of every message and `asyncapi/compatibility.rs` fails a removed property, a changed type, a lost enumeration value, a property that became required and a message that disappeared, and a message missing from the baseline. The policy, with a six-month deprecation, is `docs/event-schema-evolution.md`. Every outgoing webhook carries `X-Octopus-Schema-Version: 1`.
- **D93 — The audit trail says who changed what, who published, and what the platform did on its own.** Endpoint, subscription and connector changes, discarded and redriven messages and redrives of deliveries are audited with the actor in the transaction that makes the change; **publishing through the API is audited** (`event.published`, `api/{id}`, once per event admitted); refusals at the ingress are collapsed per endpoint and reason; the platform's own switch-offs of a subscription (too many dead in a row, too many waiting) are audited as the engine's. No record, log line, label or error carries a payload, a signature, a secret, the path or query of a URL, or a receiver's answer; `last_error` columns hold classes.
- **D94 — Two independent reviews, and what they found.** Before this feature was considered done, two reviewers who had no part in writing it read the uncommitted change, one for security and tenant isolation (18 findings) and one for correctness and crash safety (19). Four were found by both, so 33 are distinct. Neither found a break in the tenant boundary. Every finding was checked against the code before anything was changed, and the fixes were made with a test that fails without them (for most, one that was seen to fail first; the rest are covered by the mutation checks of the list below).

  *Fixed in code (25):* (1) a request with thousands of signatures in its header, or a stale one, made the control plane read and open secrets and hash a 240 KiB body once per signature per secret before authenticating anything: the header is bounded, the timestamp is judged and the body is hashed once per secret, all before a secret is read (D81); (2) the signature did not cover the type, the subject or the correlation key, which choose the triggers that fire and the waits that wake, so a proxy could change them: it does now, with a label that also keeps a delivery from passing for an event, and a secret under 16 bytes verifies nothing (D81); (3) the idempotency key of a run started by an event ignored which admission of the event it was, so an id used again after its window started nothing for as long as the key was remembered (D84); (4) event waits that existed before the migration had no start instant, which made the search for waits fail for everybody: they are given one and the search no longer depends on it (G95); (5) one consumer that could not be served (no room for its run) kept the others from theirs, and a wait for room used up an event's attempts until it was set aside after about 47 minutes, taking its payload out of reach: consumers are isolated, waiting for room is not counted and is bounded by a day (D84); (6) a delivery worker whose lease ran out could, on finally recording its failure, undo a delivery another worker had completed, and count it against the subscription: the record is fenced by the attempt number (D88); (7) a resume of a paused subscription put every dead delivery back in the queue: only a subscription the platform switched off does (D88); (8) deliveries were listed in the random order of their v4 ids, which broke the paging: v7 ids (D88); (9) a subscription that could not keep up queued deliveries for ever: it is switched off at 10 000 waiting (D88, G94); (10) a receiver that answered with a page of more than the client's limit turned a delivery into a network failure, retried twelve times and then counted against the subscription: the answer's body is never read; and a batch from SQS of more than 64 KiB wedged the connector for good: SQS reads with a limit of its own (D88, D90); (11) an SQS reader never reread its credentials or policy: it gives up after five failed calls in a row and is started again; it also waits longer between reads while it is leaving messages in the queue (D90); (12) a cron tick that could not start was re-keyed by its retry, so a start that had committed unseen could start twice, and the next tick was worked out from a replica's clock instead of the database's: the tick stays the tick under a `retry_at`, and everything follows the database's clock (D87); (13) a Kafka reader renewed its lease between fetches only, and a NATS reader let the server deliver more than it could acknowledge in time: renewed between messages, and bounded in flight (D90); (14) dead letters evicted the oldest when full, which is the evidence of what went wrong first, and counted a redelivered one again: the first thousand are kept and a repeat is not counted (D90); (15) an API-published window under ten minutes was refused by the signature rule for an event that has no signature (D80); (16) an endpoint could take the slug `cron` (D79); (17) every webhook request and every delivery read all the versions of a secret and cut the list in the program: the query is bounded (D81); (18) a stuck consumer could hold up the supervisor of every other connector: it is given 30 seconds (D90); (19) a Kafka topic could make a reader spawn a task per partition without bound: 256 (D90); (20) passwords and tokens could be sent to a server without TLS: they are not (D90); (21) the URL of a subscription, whose path can be the credential of a chat tool, was shown in full to read-only roles: it is not (D88); (22) publishing through the API was not audited and the principal was lost when the event was purged: it is (D93);
