# ADR-0083: The cron triggers are handed to the schedule pass once, and only when their schedules have converged

**Status:** Accepted (2026-11-13, OD-2026-11-13-4; proposed 2026-11-08)
**Decided by:** agent (tasks T018-060, T018-061), after the correctness, security and architecture reviews of 2026-11-08 and the third round of them; the owner has not accepted it.
**Refines / supersedes:** refines ADR-0082 (the first feature to activate through the registry) and ADR-0050 decision 9; **narrows ADR-0082 decision 2** (see below); amends `plan.md` of feature 018 ("the migration creates one trigger-owned schedule per existing cron trigger").
**Enforced by:** only checks that exist and run. `tests/integration/schedules-bulk-operations/trigger_round3.rs` (one definition of converged over every state, the cron that never fires, the pause in `LEGACY`, the order and the bound of the locks derived from `pg_locks` and a long publication, the state the function does not know, the name of the operator, the command as the binary runs it, the legacy pass held across the handover, the unreadable state), `trigger_followup.rs` (the refusal under the lock with a publication in flight, the removal guard, the control window, the lock-order deadlock test on the real code, the tripwire), `trigger_handover.rs` (owner-only, one-way, the command line), `trigger_scale.rs` (the window and the sweep over 20,000 triggers), **`trigger_plan.rs` (the fitness function of the rule that the check under the lock does not depend on the plan the statistics give it: the plan of `EXISTS` over the drift view has no scan of `schedules` or `schedule_revisions` and an index probe by `schedules_trigger_uniq` for each trigger, at 10,000 triggers never analyzed and at 5,000 analyzed before they were written, in three states of the statistics each, with the singleton never analyzed; the check answers within a second, which a machine ten times slower than the one that measured it still does; and the view lists exactly what its previous definition listed, kept in the test as an oracle, for every kind of drift, in `LEGACY` and in a rolled-back `HANDED_OVER`). There is **no** rule of `scripts/check-architecture.py` for the handover: the architecture enforcement of this decision is the tests above. The alert rule tests exist and are not yet run by CI (see Consequences).

## Context

Feature 004 fires cron triggers from the engines' legacy pass. Feature 018 makes each trigger a schedule so that an operator can pause it, and hands the firing over to the schedule pass at an operator's command. Four properties of that handover are hard to reverse or surprising without their reasons: how the schedules of the existing triggers come to exist, whether they use the tenant's schedule quota, what authority the engine has when it starts a tick, and what exactly the one-way switch requires.

## Decision

1. **No data migration makes the schedules of the existing triggers (D2033).** Publication makes the schedule of each trigger it registers, in the publishing transaction, and a reconciliation pass in every engine that runs the schedule pass makes the rest (a control plane older than the feature, triggers older than the feature). A migration cannot plan a next time (the calendar and the time-zone database are not in the schema) and would walk every tenant's rows while holding locks.
2. **Trigger-owned schedules are exempt from the `schedules` quota (D2034).** The quota is the tenant's to spend on schedules it makes; the number of triggers is bounded per workflow (16) by the workflow's own limits. A publication fails at the tenant content ceiling (the schedule and revision rows are charged to it) as it already does for the version itself; it is never refused for the schedule quota.
3. **The engine is the authority of a trigger-owned schedule and needs no membership (D2039, D2046).** `system:engine` holds no membership; the admission of a run whose schedule has a trigger and whose authority is the engine does not look one up, in every path that starts it (the tick, the release of an action that waited, a run-now whose waiting action was released). A person's backfill or run-now keeps the person's authority check. Database steps stand on the publisher, as they do for the legacy pass.
4. **"Converged" has one definition, and the handover is one-way and requires it (D2046, D2048, D2069).** The view `schedule_trigger_drift` lists the cron triggers whose schedule is not equal to them (none, a tombstone, another expression or zone, not planned, or paused while the state is `LEGACY`); the reconciliation repairs what the view lists and the handover counts it, so they cannot disagree. A calendar with no occurrence is marked exhausted and is converged. `schedule_trigger_handover_complete` is owner-only; under the requirement epoch it takes a lock on the triggers' table (no publication commits meanwhile), asks whether the view is empty and refuses unless it is; it then moves the state (only from `LEGACY`), records the operator and `session_user`, and the requirement of `schedules-v1` can never be removed again while the state is `HANDED_OVER`. There is no command to go back.
5. **The locks are taken in one order and held briefly (D2070, D2071).** The requirement epoch, the table of the cron triggers, the handover row. The table lock waits at most two seconds (`HANDOVER_BUSY`, "run the command again", nothing recorded), so that the most a writer stalls because of the command is two seconds. Under the lock the check is an `EXISTS`; the count and the ids are read outside it.
6. **The control of a trigger-owned schedule opens a few seconds after the commit (D2053, D2072).** Engines believe `LEGACY` for at most 2 s (and read the state before each trigger, not once per round); control (pause, resume, run now, backfill) is refused until `changed_at` plus 5 s by the database's clock; a unit test ties the two constants with a margin of two seconds.
7. **A round costs a window, not the number of triggers (D2072).** The reconciliation reads 2,000 triggers at a time in the order of an index, and sweeps; its writes are paced at 100 a second **at the most**: the pause that keeps to that is added to the time of the round, so the pace measured is lower: 62 a second at 100,000 triggers and 30 at 10,000 under the default settings, 77 and 69 with the per-tenant cap of a round raised to 100, never 100 (G2048, D2118).

## Narrows ADR-0082 decision 2

ADR-0082 says that the feature's step of an activation "MUST NOT take a lock that a transaction reading the epoch may hold: it reads, it does not lock". The step of this handover **does lock**: `LOCK TABLE workflow_triggers IN SHARE ROW EXCLUSIVE MODE` (bounded at two seconds) and `FOR UPDATE` on the singleton handover row. The reason is the guarantee the handover exists for: no publication of a cron trigger may commit between the count of the triggers that have no schedule and the commit of the state, or the trigger would be published after the legacy pass stopped and before the schedule pass knew of it, and nothing would fire it. The census alone cannot give that, because a publication in flight is invisible to a count. The narrowing is safe for ADR-0082's reason: **no transaction that reads the epoch (the heartbeat, a registration, `platform_requirement_lock` readers) touches `workflow_triggers` at table level or the handover row**; the only transactions that take those locks are this activation and publications and the legacy pass's moves of a trigger, and none of them holds the epoch, so none can wait for the activation while holding what the activation wants. A publication that holds the table or a row can delay the activation, never the other way round beyond the two-second bound. ADR-0082 decision 2 stands for every other step.

## Considered options

- *A data migration for the existing triggers.* Rejected for the reason in 1.
- *Count trigger-owned schedules against the quota.* Rejected: a tenant at its quota could not publish a cron workflow.
- *A reverse command.* Rejected: after the handover a trigger fired by the schedule pass may be paused; going back would resurrect the legacy pass that ignores a pause (G1011).
- *A precheck by the drift gauge.* Rejected: it excludes entries that are backing off, so a stuck entry reads zero (H); the census counts all shards in the activation's own transaction.
- *Reject a cron that never fires at publication with a typed error, as the only answer.* It is done (feature 004's calendar refuses it); it is not enough, because a row an older build wrote must not block the handover of the platform, so such a row is marked exhausted.
- *Fail closed when the handover state cannot be read.* Rejected (D2075): it would lose ticks on a fleet whose engines do not run the schedule pass; the failures are counted and alerted on instead.

## Consequences

An upgrade has a window of seconds in which a published trigger has no next time (the legacy pass fires meanwhile); a handover can be refused for a while after an upgrade (the operator waits for the reconciliation, which is paced at 100 writes a second at the most, drains at 62 a second for 100,000 triggers (27 minutes) and 30 a second for 10,000 (6 minutes) under the defaults, and at 77 and 69 with the per-tenant cap raised to 100 (21 and 2 minutes), for two tenants (G2048) and takes 20 entries of a tenant a round).

**Measured and estimated, said apart.** *Measured* (one developer machine, PostgreSQL 16 in Docker, other sessions running; `trigger_scale::a_round_costs_the_window_it_reads_and_a_sweep_finds_every_drift_over_twenty_thousand_triggers`): 20,000 triggers of 200 tenants, five with no schedule: the convergence count took 48 and 51 ms in two runs, a window of 2,000 triggers at most 63 and 66 ms, eleven windows covered every trigger (the seeding of the rows is not part of the measurement). *Measured in round 2 with the single query that the window replaced*: 100,000 triggers, drift 122 ms and count 34 ms. **Estimated, not measured:** the sweep of 100,000 triggers is 50 windows (about twelve seconds at a window every 250 ms), and a backlog of 100,000 unplanned triggers of one tenant drains in about seventeen minutes at 20 entries a round and 100 writes a second; across many tenants fewer. **That estimate was wrong, and the benchmark of 2026-11-09 corrected it (G2048, D2118; `tests/performance/reports/schedules-bulk-operations.md`, one machine, two tenants, the engine's own loop for 60 s):** the ceiling is 100 writes a second and the measured drain is 30 to 77 a second, so 100,000 triggers take **21 to 27 minutes** (27 under the default settings, 62 a second; 21 with the per-tenant cap of a round raised to 100, 77 a second), and 10,000 take 2 to 6 minutes. The convergence count took 193 ms at 100,000 unconverged triggers (6 ms at 10,000), a window 46 ms (336 ms the slowest at 100,000), and a whole sweep of 100,000 triggers 9.1 s of reads before the fix of G2049 and 2.4 s after it (50 windows of 48 ms at the median and 86 ms at the most, to which the pause of a quarter of a second between two windows is added).

**The check under the lock, measured, and what was changed (G2049, D2117).** The check the handover makes under the table lock (`schedule_trigger_unconverged()`, an `EXISTS` over the view) took 2.85 s at 10,000 triggers, 1.0 s at 5,000 and 28 ms at 15,000: the planner optimises an `EXISTS` for its first row, estimates the filter of the view to keep nearly every trigger, and estimates the singleton `schedule_trigger_handover`, which autovacuum never analyzes, at 610 rows, so it chose a nested loop with no start-up cost that scanned `schedules` again for every trigger (99,990,000 rows removed by a join filter at 10,000). The statistics were not stale (the tables were analyzed): analyzing the singleton alone brought the same query to 21 ms. The view now looks the schedule of a trigger up by `trigger_id` for each trigger (`LEFT JOIN LATERAL ... LIMIT 1` over the partial unique index) and reads the singleton by a scalar subquery, so the plan is an index probe for each trigger, and no statistic, missing or stale, offers the planner the join that it rescanned (the form does not stop it from choosing a per-trigger sequential scan with a limit, which PostgreSQL does not rule out by a partial unique index; `trigger_plan.rs` is what holds the plan, in three states of the statistics, and fails on that plan too): the check on a converged fleet took (median of three repetitions, the same benchmark, release build) 10 ms at 5,000 triggers (1,009 ms before), 20 ms at 10,000 (2,853 ms before), 28 ms at 15,000 (28 ms: the plan was the index one already), 39 ms at 20,000, 112 ms at 50,000 and 263 ms at 100,000 (280 ms before; 335 ms the slowest of the fifteen samples, 344 ms before). Under the lock the cost is now linear in the number of triggers (about 2.6 microseconds a trigger); the 4 s statement timeout would be reached at about 1.5 million triggers by extrapolation, which nobody measured (G2052).

Accepted limits, each written in the runbook (`docs/runbooks/schedules.md` 6.2 and 6.4) and in `research.md`:

- an old engine binary that never registers keeps its legacy pass after the handover and ignores a pause (G2005); the tripwire counts it only for ticks whose nominal time is after the handover plus ten seconds (the table keeps no real fire time), assumes a legacy round ends within ten seconds of the commit, and works only while one engine of this release runs the schedule pass (D2074, G2019);
- the census covers the execution engines only: a control plane older than the feature can publish a cron trigger after the handover, and its first ticks are lost until the reconciliation plans it; the operator attests that every control plane runs this build (D2072, G2020); a trigger published before the reconciliation runs has no next time until the next round;
- the handover has no tenant audit entry of its own; its record is the handover row and the registry's history, and the owner login is shared, so the database role recorded with a handover does not tell operators apart (D2042);
- a legacy pass that cannot read the state goes on firing (D2075);
- a backfill over ticks the legacy pass started more than seven days ago starts a second run (G2021);
- **no released build lists `schedules-v1`**: the registry commit is in no tag (`v0.1.0`, `v0.2.0`), so no build in the field can have listed it and revoked the engine's authority; a paused trigger schedule before the handover is an accident of this branch only (D2069).
- **CI does not run the alert rule tests of this feature or of ADR-0082**: the step `promtool test rules` of `.github/workflows/ci.yml` runs only `batch_stalled_test.yaml`. `trigger_schedules_test.yaml` and `instance_registry_test.yaml` exist and pass locally (`promtool test rules`, the image of the CI step); the line to add after `/tests/batch_stalled_test.yaml` is ` /tests/instance_registry_test.yaml /tests/trigger_schedules_test.yaml` (G2022). The workflow is not touched by this change because a change of it reruns the whole stack.
