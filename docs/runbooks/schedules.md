# Runbook: schedules (feature 018)

For the people who run the platform and for operators who answer "why did, or did not, this schedule start a run". It says how a schedule is turned on, what the engine does with it, what to look at when it misbehaves, and what is not built yet.

Design: [ADR-0050](../adr/ADR-0050-many-run-operations-admit-under-stable-keys-and-never-buffer-or-reopen.md); decisions, gaps and owner questions: [`specs/018-schedules-bulk-operations/research.md`](../../specs/018-schedules-bulk-operations/research.md). The batch half of the feature is in [`batch-operations.md`](batch-operations.md).

## 1. What a schedule is

A schedule starts a run of a published workflow at the times its rule gives. The rule (the *spec*) is an interval, cron expressions or calendars, in an IANA time zone, with an optional start, end and jitter. The platform, not the browser and not a worker, decides every time, on the **database clock**.

- **Who it acts for.** Every run is started as `system:schedule:<id>` with the role of the person who last wrote the schedule (`authorizedBy`), and that person's permission to start runs is checked again, in the transaction that admits the run, every time. A schedule whose author lost `run:start` stops starting runs: the time is decided `AUTHORITY_REVOKED` and the schedule pauses itself with a system note (`schedule.auto_paused`).
- **What a time can become.** Every nominal time of a schedule has exactly one decision, whether or not it started a run (one exception, in section 1.1: the time that falls due while a waiting scheduled action keeps the last remaining action of a schedule that has a limit): `STARTED`, `BUFFERED` (waiting for a run to end), `SKIPPED_OVERLAP`, `DROPPED_BUFFER_FULL`, `DROPPED_DELETED`, `COALESCED` (several late times merged into one summary), `MISSED_CATCHUP_WINDOW`, `ALREADY_ACTED` (a backfill found it done, or an open run that holds the business id of the action answered it: section 1.1), `AUTHORITY_REVOKED`, `BUSINESS_ID_CONFLICT` (the business id of the action was held and the policy refused it: section 1.1). They are listed newest first at `GET /namespaces/{id}/schedules/{id}/actions` and on the schedule's page in the Studio.
- **When a run is still open** (`overlap`): `SKIP`, `BUFFER_ONE`, `BUFFER_ALL`, `CANCEL_OTHER`, `ALLOW_ALL`. `CANCEL_OTHER` cancels the open run through the ordinary cancel, so only somebody who may cancel runs (`run:operate`: admin, operator) can write it.
- **After an outage** (`catchupMode`, `catchupWindowMs`, 366 days by default): `ONE` starts only the latest missed time, `ALL` starts every one inside the window, and the rest are summarized (`COALESCED`, `MISSED_CATCHUP_WINDOW`). A recovery is planned once, remembered on the schedule (`recovery_until`, `recovery_acts`) and carried out a step at a time, so a long outage is worked through over several rounds and never by one schedule holding the others back.
- **Pause, resume, run now, backfill.** Pausing acts on no time and catches none up afterwards. *Run now* decides one time at once, as the schedule would (`origin` `MANUAL`: it never shares an identity with a scheduled time, takes no remaining action, and works while the schedule is paused). A *backfill* acts on the times of a past range (at most 1,000), one backfill at a time per schedule; a time that already has a decision is never given a second run.
- **Permissions.** `schedule:read` (every human role); `schedule:write` with `run:start` to create, change or delete (admin, developer); `schedule:operate` to pause (admin, developer, operator); resuming, running now and backfilling need `schedule:operate` and `run:start`.

### 1.1 The business id of the runs of a schedule (`FR-018-016`)

An action may name a **prefix** (`businessIdPrefix`, 1 to 160 visible ASCII characters), with a conflict policy (`businessIdConflictPolicy`: `FAIL`, the default, or `USE_EXISTING`) and a reuse policy (`businessIdReusePolicy`: `ALLOW_DUPLICATE`, the default, `ALLOW_DUPLICATE_FAILED_ONLY`, `REJECT_DUPLICATE`), the two of a person's start ([`business-ids.md`](business-ids.md)). The run an action starts is bound to the id `<prefix>-<nominal time>`: the nominal time in RFC 3339, UTC, to the **whole second**, ending in `Z` (`order-2026-11-10T12:00:00Z`; the id never depends on the clock that read the instant, which PostgreSQL keeps to the microsecond). *Run now* takes the second of the request; a backfill, the nominal time it acts on; a schedule a cron trigger owns has no prefix. The id is claimed in the transaction that admits the run, after the schedule's row and before any run's (the lock order of the plan, row 2; the schedule's row is held while the claim waits for a competing claim of the same id), so a time acted on twice claims once, and a crash at any write leaves nothing and the retry makes one run and one binding.

What a time becomes when the id is taken, decided right after the claim (the claim's transaction is rolled back so that the binding's row is not held, the subject the schedule acts for is looked at again under the lock of its membership first, and the decision commits in a transaction of its own under the same shard fence and the same compare-and-swap on the schedule: a crash between the two leaves nothing and the next pass decides again) and recorded as the decision of the time (never retried: a held id is the answer of the policy, not a lack of room, so it is not an `ADMISSION_RETRY`; no time is owed, the schedule goes on to the next one, `remainingActions` is not used up, nothing pauses the schedule, and no run is tracked):

| The id is held by | Policy | The decision | `reason` | `runId` |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| an open run (or a chain not yet final) | `FAIL` | `BUSINESS_ID_CONFLICT` | `BUSINESS_ID_IN_USE` | none |
| an open run | `USE_EXISTING` | `ALREADY_ACTED` | `BUSINESS_ID_IN_USE` | the run that holds it (the current segment of a chain) |
| a closed run inside the window (30 days) | `REJECT_DUPLICATE`, or `ALLOW_DUPLICATE_FAILED_ONLY` after a success | `BUSINESS_ID_CONFLICT` | `BUSINESS_ID_REUSE_REJECTED` | none |
| a closed run inside the window | `ALLOW_DUPLICATE`, or `ALLOW_DUPLICATE_FAILED_ONLY` after a failure | `STARTED`: the binding is taken over by the new run | | the new run |
| a closed run past its window, or nobody | any | `STARTED` | | the new run |

An action that waited for a run to end (`BUFFER_*`, `CANCEL_OTHER`) claims its id when it is released, not when it was buffered: if the id is held by then, the waiting row becomes the decision above (one row, not two) and the schedule's count of waiting actions goes down. Counters: `schedule_actions_total{kind,outcome}` counts `business_id_conflict` and `already_acted` like any outcome; **no label, log line, span, audit reason, outbox payload or journal event carries the prefix or the id** (they are customer text, kept in the binding, on the run, and, for the prefix, in the schedule's revision), and the reasons above are closed codes. The Studio's schedule page and `sagat schedule get`/`actions` show the prefix and the reasons.

**One id space, per second.** The id of *run now* is `<prefix>` and the second of the request, and a scheduled time's is `<prefix>` and its nominal time: a *run now* in the second of a scheduled time of the same schedule (or of a schedule with the same prefix) makes the same id, and whichever commits second finds it held (under `FAIL` the scheduled time is then recorded `BUSINESS_ID_CONFLICT` and never starts a run). A schedule whose times are on the second and that is run by hand in that second is the case; run by hand, or wait for the next second.

When a *waiting* **scheduled** action (`BUFFER_*`, `CANCEL_OTHER`) that kept the last remaining action for itself is settled because its id was held, the action it kept is given back and the schedule goes on from the first time after the settling; **the time that fell due while it waited was passed over without a decision** (the one exception to "every nominal time has exactly one decision": a schedule with nothing left to give records nothing, and this one had given its last action to the waiting one; D2189). A waiting *run now* or *backfill* step keeps no remaining action, because neither uses one up (`remainingActions` is decremented only by an action of the schedule that starts a run): a time that falls due while one waits is decided like any other, and nothing has to be given back (D2190).

**What a waiting action is made under.** An action that waited is made under the schedule **as it is when it is released**: the prefix and its two policies, like the workflow, the version policy and the input, are the head revision's, not the ones of the revision it was buffered under (`D2186`). Its row in the history keeps the revision it was decided under. Update the schedule while an action waits and the run it makes is under the new prefix.

**Upgrading: the engines first (G2091).** An execution engine built before the business-id prefix existed does not know it: when it holds the shard of a schedule it starts the run **with no business id**, no claim and no conflict decision, and nothing says so. Upgrade every engine before the first schedule with a prefix is written, as for the other flags of this feature (section 2). A policy (`businessIdConflictPolicy`, `businessIdReusePolicy`) with no prefix is a `422 SCHEDULE_SPEC_INVALID` (`details.field` `/action/businessIdConflictPolicy` or `/action/businessIdReusePolicy`) where an older control plane accepted it and dropped it.

To find who holds an id an action found taken: `GET /namespaces/{id}/business-ids/{id}` (needs `run:read`; the id is the prefix, a dash and the nominal time of the decision, which the decision lists). A prefix that is not an id is refused when the schedule is written (`422` `SCHEDULE_SPEC_INVALID`, `details.field` `/action/businessIdPrefix`, the text never echoed), a policy without a prefix too.

## 2. Turning it on

Off by default, in two places that must agree. Turn it on in the engines first and then in the control plane, and turn it off in the control plane first (the order of ADR-0081, which the registry of ADR-0050 decision 9 will replace):

| Where | Setting | Default | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| control plane | `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_ENABLED` | `false` | serves the schedule routes (otherwise `404`) |
| engine | `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_ENABLED` | `false` | fires the schedules of the shards the replica holds |
| engine | `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_BATCH` | 100 (1 to 5,000) | due schedules looked at in a round |
| engine | `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_ROUND_ACTIONS` | 100 (1 to 1,000) | steps one round takes over all its schedules and backfills; the next round carries on |
| engine | `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_POLL_MS` | 1000 | pause when a round found nothing |

The development stack passes all of these from `infra/docker/.env` to both services, so one line turns a setting on in both places (`SAGAT_SCHEDULES_ENABLED=true`; see section 1 of the [quickstart of the feature](../../specs/018-schedules-bulk-operations/quickstart.md#1-prerequisites)).

With the routes on and the engine off, schedules can be written and never fire: the lag gauge (section 4) grows. A round shares its `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_BATCH` between the tenants that have schedules due the same way as the passes of the runs ([`scaling.md`](scaling.md)), so one tenant's backlog does not hold back the others; `SAGAT_SCHEDULER_DISCOVERY_REST_MS` rests the search for other tenants.

Limits a namespace can change (`quota_ceilings`, `quota_limits`): the number of `schedules` (200 by default) and of `schedule_backfills` running at once (4). In the spec: at most 10 entries together (hard 32), a minimum spacing of 60 s (floor 10 s) over the next 1,000 times, jitter at most 24 hours, an input of at most 64 KiB.

## 3. When a schedule did not start a run

Open the schedule in the Studio (`/schedules`), or read its decisions and describe it over REST. In this order:

1. **Is it acting at all?** `state` `PAUSED` (look at the note and who wrote it), or *exhausted* (`remainingActions` reached zero), or past its `endAt`: there is no next time. A schedule that paused itself says why in its note (`the authorizing subject can no longer start runs`, or `a run of the schedule failed` when `pauseOnFailure` is set).
2. **Is there a decision for the time?** If yes, its outcome and reason say it: `SKIPPED_OVERLAP`/`BUFFERED` (a run it started is still open: `openRuns` on the schedule, which lists the runs still open when it is read), `TRACKING_LIMIT` (it already tracks as many open runs as it may), `BUSINESS_ID_IN_USE` / `BUSINESS_ID_REUSE_REJECTED` (the business id of the action was held or remembered: section 1.1; the holder is the run `GET .../business-ids/<id>` names, or the `runId` of an `ALREADY_ACTED`), `NO_PUBLISHED_VERSION` (the workflow has nothing to start; the time stays owed and is retried with a growing delay), `AUTHORITY_REVOKED`, `COALESCED`/`MISSED_CATCHUP_WINDOW` (it was too late: after an outage, or created with a start in the past).
3. **If there is no decision and the time has passed**, the engine did not get to it: is `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_ENABLED` on in the engine, does a replica hold the schedule's shard (`scheduler_shards_owned` sums to 64), is `scheduler_lag_seconds{pass="schedules"}` high (a burst of schedules due together makes it high for as long as one replica needs to start them all, and a namespace at its quota of active runs makes it grow without end: section 4.1)? The decision is made in the database transaction that starts the run, so a replica that lost its lease is refused where it commits and the new owner does it: a time is never started twice.
4. **A run was started and then nothing happened to it**: that run is an ordinary run; see [`execution-engine.md`](execution-engine.md).

**Which schedule made this run?** The run says so itself. `GET /runs/{id}` and `GET /runs` carry, for a run a schedule started, `schedule {scheduleId, nominalTime, kind, revision}`: the schedule, the nominal time it calculated (UTC, to the microsecond; for *run now* the instant of the action), the kind of action (`SCHEDULED`, `TRIGGERED` for *run now*, `BACKFILL`) and the revision of the schedule the action was taken under. The object is absent, not `null`, for every other run. A run a reset made has, in the same way, `resetFrom {runId, sequence}` (and `rerunOf`, which a plain *rerun as new* has alone). The Studio's run page says it in its header ("Started by schedule *name* (kind), nominal time …", with a link to the schedule), `sagat workflow inspect <run>` prints it in words, and the six SDKs have it as `Run.schedule` and `Run.resetFrom`. When the run finishes, the `run.finished` webhook and broker body of a run a schedule started carries `data.scheduleId` and `data.nominalTime` (nothing is added for another run, and the hint on NATS or Redis is unchanged); no event carries a business identifier. What it does **not** say: a run the legacy cron pass started for a trigger before the handover (it writes no provenance: its `startedBy` is `system:engine`), and the later segments of a continuation chain whose first run a schedule started (only the first run has it).

`schedule_actions_total{kind,outcome}` counts decisions (and `outcome="admission_retry"` the times the admission refused and left owed); `schedule_action_lag_seconds{kind}` is how long after its time a scheduled action started.

## 4. Watching it

| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `scheduler_lag_seconds{pass="schedules"}` | how long the oldest active schedule has been due; `SagatSchedulerLate` fires above 60 s for five minutes, for this pass as for the others ([`scaling.md`](scaling.md#scheduler-late)) |
| `schedule_actions_total{kind,outcome}` | decisions, counted once they are committed; a decision the fence refused counts nothing |
| `schedule_action_lag_seconds{kind}` | firing lag of the scheduled actions |
| `scheduler_fenced_total{kind="schedule"}` and `{kind="backfill"}` | steps another replica or a lost lease took away |
| `scheduler_fairness_limited_total{pass="schedules"}` | tenants given less than they showed, because another had a backlog |

No label is a tenant, a schedule or a run. The audit trail records `schedule.created`, `updated`, `deleted`, `paused`, `resumed`, `auto_paused`, `triggered`, `backfill_requested` and `backfill_cancelled` in the transaction of the change, and `business_id.conflict` (by the schedule, with the closed reason and never the id) in the transaction of a decision that found its id held, never the input and never a note (a pause or resume note, who wrote it and when are on the schedule: `note`, `noteBy`, `noteAt`).

### 4.1 Capacity: how many schedules can be due together

**These are measurements of one machine on one day, not promises** (NFR-018-005 says the firing lag, the batch rate, the business-id claim and the reset time are "measured on a declared environment; nothing is promised"). The machine: an Apple M3 Max, PostgreSQL 16 in a Docker Desktop VM of 16 CPUs and 7.75 GiB (`fsync` on; one commit 0.5 ms), the release build, the runtime role, the engine's own loops with their default settings, no worker, no HTTP in front, one tenant, a machine that ran other work at a load of 3 to 13. The report, its method and its failures: [`tests/performance/reports/schedules-bulk-operations.md`](../../tests/performance/reports/schedules-bulk-operations.md). Another machine, a quiet one, a database on another host or runs that execute will give other numbers: measure yours before you size by these.

**One engine replica started about 61 runs a second** (60 to 64) from schedules due at the same instant, so **a burst of N schedules due together is late by about N / 61 seconds at its last, and about half that on average**. An action costs about 16 ms, of which the commit is 0.5 ms. A second replica on the same database held other shards and about doubled the rate (131 to 135 a second). Lag of the action behind its nominal time, median of three repetitions each (p95 over the actions of a repetition):

| Schedules | Due | Replicas | Started a second | Lag p50 | Lag p95 | Lag, the last |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1,000 | at the same instant | 1 | 64 | 8.2 s | 15.3 s | 16.2 s |
| 10,000 | at the same instant | 1 | 61 | 82 s | 157 s | 165 s |
| 1,000 | at the same instant | 2 | 131 | 4.2 s | 7.6 s | 8.2 s |
| 10,000 | at the same instant | 2 | 135 | 37 s | 70 s | 74 s |
| 1,000 | spread over a minute (17 a second) | 1 | 17 | 0.5 s | 1.0 s | 1.2 s |
| 10,000 | spread over a minute (167 a second) | 1 | 60 | 52 s | 100 s | 106 s |

What follows from it, each as measured or as an extrapolation that nobody measured:

- *Measured:* a fleet that arrives at a rate under what a replica starts is kept up with (1,000 per-minute schedules, p95 1.0 s); one that arrives faster than that is not, and its lag grows until the arrivals stop (10,000 per-minute schedules arrive at 167 a second against 61: p95 100 s). *Extrapolated, not measured:* about three replicas or more for 10,000 schedules of a per-minute spec.
- *Measured:* the ceilings of a round are not a throughput lever. `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_ROUND_ACTIONS` 1,000 with `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_BATCH` 500 gave the same 157 s at the p95 and 60.6 actions a second, with rounds of 8.3 s instead of 1.6 s: a ceiling bounds the length of a round and the search for fairness between tenants, not the rate.
- *Measured:* `SagatSchedulerLate` (`scheduler_lag_seconds` above 60 s for five minutes) would not have fired in any repetition, because the gauge was above 60 s for 110 s at the most, though a gauge the replicas scale on would have moved; a burst is a lag that ends by itself, and the alert is for the one that does not.
- *Not measured:* more than one tenant (the fairness between tenants of the schedule pass), the HTTP API, workers that run what is started, and a database on another machine (G2050).

**The quota of active runs.** A namespace admits 10,000 active runs by default (`active_runs`, and `tenant_active_runs` for a tenant; how to read and raise them: [`security-governance.md`](security-governance.md)). A burst of schedules whose runs stay open (long runs, or no worker, as in the benchmark) stops at it: the first launch of the benchmark with 10,000 schedules stopped at 9,998 acted. The pass does not fail and no time is lost: the admission is refused, **the time stays owed and is tried again with a growing delay** (`ADMISSION_RETRY`), and the pass goes on trying, without end, while the namespace stays at its quota (10,500 refusals in 426 s in the benchmark). What you see: `schedule_actions_total{outcome="admission_retry"}` rising, `scheduler_lag_seconds{pass="schedules"}` and `schedule_action_lag_seconds` growing, and `SagatSchedulerLate` firing on that lag, not on its cause. Look at the namespace's `active_runs` before you look at the pass; the cure is for runs to end or for the quota to be raised on purpose (the benchmark raises it for every scenario).

## 5. The input

The input of a schedule is charged content. An ordinary read **never returns it**: it gives its SHA-256 digest only, a change must send it whole, and the Studio asks for it again on every edit.

Its values come out through one route, `POST /namespaces/{namespaceId}/schedules/{scheduleId}/input-disclosures`, which needs `schedule:read` and applies the audiences of feature 016 to the input as `POST /runs/{runId}/payload-disclosures` does to a run's: `public` values to every reader, `internal` ones with `payload:read-internal`, `pii` ones only when the request says `{"reveal": ["pii"]}` and the caller has the namespace's reveal grant (otherwise `403` `PII_REVEAL_NOT_GRANTED`), and `secret` ones never. The classification is that of the input schema of the version the schedule would start now: the version it pins, or the workflow's active one. A workflow with no schema to classify by gives its structure to a caller with `run:payload:inspect` and nothing to the others. Every answer is `private, no-store`, and the read is on record before the values leave: `schedule.input_disclosed` for what an internal reader or an inspector got (the same read made again within five minutes is one record), and `payload.pii_revealed` for each reveal of personal data (never collapsed). A reader who sees only public values leaves no record. Look at the trail with `GET /audit?action=schedule.input_disclosed`.

## 6. Schedules of cron triggers, and the handover

Every **cron trigger** of a published workflow is also a schedule with `origin` `TRIGGER` (feature 018, US07), listed and described like any other (`?origin=TRIGGER`; its `triggerId` names the trigger). It exists so that an operator can see the trigger and, after the handover below, pause it without republishing. **It fires exactly as the trigger always did.**

- **What it is.** One schedule per cron trigger of the version in force, named `trigger-<32 hex digits of the trigger id>`, with the trigger's expression and zone, overlap `ALLOW_ALL` (every tick starts, whatever is open: the platform never tracks these runs), catch-up `ONE` over 366 days, no jitter, daylight saving as feature 004 has it (a time that does not exist is skipped, one that happens twice fires once), and the engine (`system:engine`) as its authority: nothing can revoke it. It is not counted against the namespace's `schedules` quota and never refused by it.
- **What starts a run.** The tick has the identity feature 004 gave it: scope `trigger:<trigger id>`, key `cron-<epoch seconds of the nominal time>`, the `cron.fired` event as the input (`{"event": {"source": "cron", "type": "cron.fired", ...}, "data": {"scheduledAt", "firedAt"}}`), `startedBy` `system:engine`, the version the trigger names (the version in force). While the legacy cron pass still runs, **both passes may fire a tick: one run starts, and the pass that comes second records the decision `ALREADY_ACTED`** with the run it found. A backfill of such a schedule keeps that key, so a time the legacy pass started is counted `ALREADY_ACTED` and never started again. *Run now* starts the same `cron.fired` document with `scheduledAt` and `firedAt` both now, under the manual identity of the schedule.
- **Who changes it.** Publication, in the same transaction that registers the triggers: a new trigger gets its schedule; a republication that keeps the trigger (same workflow, expression and zone: the id is a hash of them) keeps the schedule, its place and its pause; a dropped trigger removes the schedule (a tombstone, its waiting actions `DROPPED_DELETED`, its history kept); a trigger that comes back is **the same schedule** again (the unique index on the trigger spans tombstones); a changed expression or zone is **another trigger**, so another schedule. Its spec and name can never be edited (`409 SCHEDULE_TRIGGER_OWNED`) and it cannot be deleted by the API.
- **The reconciliation pass** (every engine that runs the schedule pass, every five seconds) covers what publication did not: a control plane older than this feature that published during the upgrade, the cron triggers that existed before the feature (it replaces a data migration: a migration cannot plan a next time without the calendar), a trigger whose schedule could not be made, drift of expression, zone or next time, and a schedule whose trigger an older control plane dropped. It runs while the state is `LEGACY` and after the handover, paced at **100 writes a second at the most**: the pause that keeps to it is added to the time of the round, so the pace is lower (measured on one machine with two tenants: 62 a second at 100,000 triggers and 30 at 10,000 under the default settings, 77 and 69 with the per-tenant cap of a round raised to 100; step 3 of 6.2). A round reads a window of 2,000 triggers (a range of an index, in the order of the shard and the id) and then the next window, so its cost is the window and what it writes and not the number of triggers there are: a hundred thousand triggers are swept in about fifty windows, a quarter of a second apart, so a trigger an older control plane published is found within one sweep (about fifteen seconds at a hundred thousand: 2.4 s of reads, measured, and fifty pauses of a quarter of a second) plus the five-second poll. It is bounded per round and per tenant (20 entries of a tenant, at most 500 and by default 200 in all), an entry that fails is left out for 2 s doubling to 5 min, an entry another process got to first for one second, and every log line names the tenant and the trigger. A schedule is given its first time by this pass, within seconds of the publication; until then it has no next time (a trigger published by a control plane older than the feature loses its first ticks until the pass finds it, G2020).
- **What counts as equal.** The reconciliation and the handover read one definition (the view `schedule_trigger_drift`): a trigger is *not* converged when it has no schedule, a tombstone, a schedule whose expression or zone is not the trigger's, an active schedule that lacks its nominal or its actual next time and is not exhausted, or **a paused schedule while the state is `LEGACY`** (before the handover nothing may pause one, so it is an accident, and the reconciliation resumes it). A cron the calendar can never fire (`0 0 31 2 *`) is refused at publication, as feature 004 always did; if such a row exists (an older build wrote it) its schedule is marked **exhausted** (no next time, no actions left) once, with a warning naming the trigger, and that is converged: one such row does not hold the handover of the whole platform.

### 6.1 Before the handover

While the cron triggers still fire from the legacy pass (`schedule_trigger_handover.state = LEGACY`, the state of every deployment until the command below is run), pause, resume, run now and backfill of a trigger-owned schedule are refused `409 SCHEDULE_TRIGGER_HANDOVER_PENDING`: the legacy pass would ignore the pause. Nothing else is different.

### 6.2 The handover, step by step

`control-plane schedule-trigger-handover` is the first **activation of the instance-capability registry** ([`instance-registry.md`](instance-registry.md)): in one transaction it requires `schedules-v1` of every live execution engine and moves the state to `HANDED_OVER`. **After it the legacy cron pass of every engine stops** (within about two seconds: a handover is read at most that often), the schedule pass fires the triggers, and the schedules accept pause, resume, run now and backfill. The command has no reverse (see 6.4).

1. **Upgrade everything first, and attest it.** Every control plane and every engine runs this release or later (the registry exists in them). **Attest from the orchestrator** (the images of the pods, not the registry) that no engine older than the registry release runs anywhere: such a binary never registers, so the registry cannot show it, it only makes the count `N` look smaller than the engines you run. Check `max(serving_instances_live{kind="EXECUTION_ENGINE"})` is the number of engines you run. **Attest the control planes too:** the registry census covers the execution engines only, so a control plane older than this feature that publishes a cron workflow after the handover leaves its trigger without a schedule until the reconciliation finds it, and **its first ticks are lost** (D2072, G2020): every control plane runs this release before you hand over.
2. **Turn the schedule pass on in every engine**: `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_ENABLED=true` (an engine lists `schedules-v1` only when it runs the schedule pass, so one without it blocks the handover, by name, and, after it, refuses to run at all). Then in the control plane, as section 2 says.
3. **Apply the migrations** with the owner role (`control-plane migrate`): `20270114000000_trigger_handover` makes the functions the command calls and the indexes the reconciliation reads by.
   **Convergence is 0:** wait until `max(trigger_schedule_unconverged)` is `0` (the cron triggers that have no schedule equal to them: right after an upgrade the reconciliation makes them at 100 writes a second **at the most**; measured with two tenants and the engine's own loop for 60 s, at 100,000 triggers it made 62 a second under the default settings (about 27 minutes) and 77 a second with the per-tenant cap of a round raised from 20 to 100 (about 21 minutes), and at 10,000 triggers 30 a second (about 6 minutes) and 69 (about 2); the cap is not a setting of the engine, the benchmark raised it in code, and the smaller backlog is the slower under the defaults because its sweep reaches the end of the triggers more often and a round that finds nothing to write is followed by the poll of 5 s; the pace of one tenant alone, which is limited to 20 entries a round, was not measured (G2048, G2050)). The handover refuses while it is not zero, and the dry run says how many and which. (The count includes a schedule that was paused before the handover: look for the engine build that did it; the reconciliation resumes it.) `trigger_schedule_drift` is **not** a precheck: it leaves out the entries that are backing off, so a stuck trigger reads zero there.
4. **Dry run**, with the owner role (the one `migrate` and `provision-tenant` use; the runtime role cannot run it): `control-plane schedule-trigger-handover --dry-run --expect-live N` with `N` the number of engines you run. It verifies everything (the engines, `schedules-v1`, the convergence of the triggers, the privilege) and changes nothing; it exits 0 with `dry run: nothing stops the handover`, or refuses with the reason. A command without `--apply` is a dry run too, and says so.
5. **Run it**: `control-plane schedule-trigger-handover --operator <your name> --expect-live N --apply` (on the development stack the service `migrate` runs the control-plane image with the owner role: `docker compose -f infra/docker/compose.yaml --env-file infra/docker/.env run --rm --no-deps migrate schedule-trigger-handover --operator <your name> --expect-live 1 --apply`). `--apply` is required: **the handover has no way back**. `--operator` is recorded as the actor, with the database role of the session (`session_user`), in the handover row and in the registry's history (`platform_requirement_history`: action `ACTIVATED`, capability `schedules-v1`); it must be printable (no control, bidirectional or zero-width character) and never starts with `-`. **Use one database login per operator:** the name is what was typed and `session_user` is the owner's login, the same for everyone who shares it, so a shared login gives no individual accountability. The command logs the OS user and host that ran it. `--expect-live N` makes the command refuse unless exactly `N` engines are live (seen within 30 s): an activation that finds none would pass vacuously, so **without `--expect-live` it refuses when none is live**, and `--expect-live 0` is the explicit way to say you mean it.
6. **Check**: the command prints `handed over: ...`; the control of the schedules (pause, resume, run now, backfill) opens five seconds after the commit (the engines may believe `LEGACY` for up to two seconds), and until then it is refused `SCHEDULE_TRIGGER_HANDOVER_PENDING`; `SELECT state, changed_by, changed_db_role, changed_at FROM schedule_trigger_handover` says `HANDED_OVER`; `cron_fires_total` stops rising in every engine while `schedule_actions_total{kind="scheduled"}` carries the ticks; a trigger-owned schedule can be paused.

**What the command holds, and for how long.** Inside its transaction it takes a lock on the cron triggers' table, so that no publication commits between the count and the commit of the state. It waits for the publications in flight for **at most two seconds** and, while it waits, holds back the writers that come after it (a publication, the legacy cron pass's moving of a trigger) for no longer than that: if a publication is still open after two seconds the command stops with `a publication, or a cron pass, was writing the cron triggers ... run the command again`, nothing is recorded, and the writers go on. The lock is released when the transaction ends, within a fraction of a second after it has the lock. So the most any writer stalls is about two seconds, once per attempt (D2070).

A second run says `already handed over (by <operator> as database role <role>)` and exits 0.

### 6.3 When the command refuses

| It says | Do |
|---|---|
| `N live execution engine(s) do not list schedules-v1 (instance ids: ...)` | Those engines do not run the schedule pass. Start them with `SAGAT_SCHEDULES_ENABLED=true` (or stop them), wait ~30 s for the rows of a stopped engine to expire, and run again. Nothing was changed. |
| `N cron trigger(s) have no schedule or no next time (trigger ids: ...)` | The reconciliation has not made their schedules yet (right after an upgrade or a publication by an older control plane), or it cannot: look in the engines' logs for the trigger ids (`a trigger schedule could not be reconciled`, `no schedule could be made`). Wait for `trigger_schedule_unconverged` to reach `0` and run again. Nothing was changed. |
| `N execution engine(s) are live ... and --expect-live says M` | Fewer: an engine has not registered yet or is down, **or it is an engine binary older than the registry release, which never registers and so can only show as fewer** (see step 1 of 6.2: attest it from the orchestrator). More: an engine you did not count is registered, of this release or later. Find it before handing over. |
| `no execution engine is live` | The engines are down, or not registering. Start them and run again, or pass `--expect-live 0` if you mean it. |
| `permission denied ... schema-owning database role` | You ran it with the runtime role. Use the owner role's `DATABASE_URL`. |
| (a requirement of `schedules-v1` recorded by hand, the state still `LEGACY`) | Nothing to do: the command runs the same checks and completes the handover in one transaction; if a check fails it refuses with the reason and changes nothing. |
| `the number of live execution engines changed while the handover was being made` | An engine registered or went silent between the check and the lock. Run it again. |
| `a publication, or a cron pass, was writing the cron triggers and the handover waited two seconds for it` | A publication was open (or a legacy pass was writing). Nothing was changed and nothing was held for long; run it again. |
| `the handover state is ..., which this build does not know` | A build newer than the one you run wrote a state this one cannot move. Run the command with that build. |
| `N cron trigger(s) have no schedule or no next time (trigger ids: ...)` with a trigger that was paused | A schedule was paused before the handover (an accident: nothing may pause one then). The reconciliation resumes it within a sweep; run again. |

### 6.4 Rollback, and what an old engine does (G1011)

There is no command to hand the triggers back: the table refuses any change that moves a handed-over row, and the schema `down` refuses once it happened. **The hazard:** a binary older than the registry release does not register and does not read the state, so after the handover it would keep its legacy cron pass. It would still never start a tick twice (the key is shared), but it **ignores a pause**: a paused trigger-owned schedule would go on firing from that engine. An engine of this release or later without `schedules-v1` refuses to run instead (registration is refused and its whole gate closes), which is why the handover waits for every live engine to list it. So: do not start a binary older than the registry release after the handover, and do not roll the engines back below it; if you must, rollback the schema only in a quiesced service after reading `down`.

**What the platform accepts, and says so (D2072, G2020 to G2022).**

- The census covers the execution engines; a control plane older than this feature can publish a cron trigger after the handover, and its first ticks are lost until the reconciliation plans it (a sweep and the poll, seconds, longer under a backlog). Step 1 of 6.2 asks you to attest the control planes. `SagatTriggersUnconvergedAfterHandover` fires after five minutes.
- A trigger published before the reconciliation has run has no next time until the next round, in `LEGACY` as well.
- The handover has no tenant audit entry of its own: its record is the handover row and the registry's history (D2042).
- **A backfill of a trigger-owned schedule over ticks the legacy pass started more than seven days ago starts a second run** for them: the legacy pass left no action row, only a deduplication key, and keys are purged after seven days. Backfill windows of trigger-owned schedules should not reach before the handover and 7 days (G2021).
- **A legacy cron pass that cannot read whether the handover happened goes on firing**, because failing closed would lose ticks on a fleet whose engines do not run the schedule pass; it counts the failures (`cron_handover_read_failures_total`, `cron_handover_read_consecutive_failures`) and `SagatCronHandoverUnreadable` fires after five in a row for two minutes. A pause made after the five-second window is honoured by every engine that can read the state.
- The tripwire for an old engine binary sees a tick only when its nominal time is later than the handover plus ten seconds (the table keeps no real fire time), and only while one engine of this release runs the schedule pass (G2019).

### 6.5 Watching the reconciliation

| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `trigger_schedule_unconverged` | cron triggers with no schedule equal to them, over every shard (measured every sixth round that ends a sweep, by each replica: read it with `max`). **The precheck of the handover**; `SagatTriggersUnconverged` fires above 0 for 30 minutes, and `SagatTriggersUnconvergedAfterHandover` for 5 minutes once `trigger_schedule_handed_over` is 1 |
| `trigger_schedule_backing_off` | entries a replica leaves out of its rounds after a failure; one that never leaves is a stuck trigger (find it in the logs by tenant and trigger) |
| `trigger_schedule_last_round_timestamp_seconds` | when a replica last completed a round, **including a replica that holds no shard** (its round has nothing to do and the loop is alive); `SagatTriggerScheduleReconcileStale` fires when it is older than 60 s on an engine that runs the schedule pass (the gauge exists only where the pass runs, that is, where `schedules-v1` is listed) |
| `trigger_schedule_reconcile_failures_total`, `trigger_schedule_rows_undecodable_total` | failed rounds and entries, and rows this build cannot read; `SagatTriggerScheduleReconcileFailing` fires when the first keeps rising for 15 minutes |
| `trigger_schedules_unmade_total{reason}` | a trigger whose schedule could not be made (`names_taken`) |
| `cron_legacy_fires_after_handover_total{source="legacy_pass"}` | a legacy cron pass fired a trigger after the handover settled: an engine binary older than the registry release is running (G2005). Counted from the first look of each replica after it started (history is not replayed after a restart), so several replicas count one fire several times: alert on `increase(...) > 0`, which `SagatCronLegacyFiredAfterHandover` does. Find the engine and stop it |
| `trigger_schedule_handed_over` | 1 when the replica read the state `HANDED_OVER` |
| `cron_handover_read_failures_total`, `cron_handover_read_consecutive_failures` | reads of the handover state by the legacy cron pass that failed, and the run of failures now; `SagatCronHandoverUnreadable` fires at 5 for 2 minutes |

## 7. What is not built yet

- The six SDKs keep the field `controllable` of a trigger-owned schedule reachable in the raw resource and have no typed field for it (G2009).
- The **input schema** of the workflow is not checked at write or at action (G1955: the platform enforces `inputSchema` nowhere, so a schedule that checked it would refuse what a person's start accepts).
- A **preview** of a backfill (G1966) and the validation of the input against the input schema of the workflow (G1955).
- Replay of a write by its `Idempotency-Key` (G1014): a write sent again after a lost answer is refused by what the first did (`409` for a name in use or a stale `expectedRevision`), and nothing is applied twice.
- The schedules, decisions and revisions of a deleted schedule are kept; their retention is G1954. Old decisions are purged by the maintenance pass, never one that waits.
